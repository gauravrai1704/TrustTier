\begin{abstract}
Decentralized finance (DeFi) lending protocols liquidate a borrower only once the loan-to-value ratio crosses a fixed threshold. In a crash this acts too late, after a position is already insolvent. A risk model that also sees volatility and momentum can act earlier, with a smaller soft liquidation, but its verdicts usually come from an operator nobody can audit. Zero-knowledge machine learning (zkML) can prove that a verdict came from a committed model, yet a proof-verified decision costs about 1.0\,M gas, too much to pay for every position.

TrustTier spends verification only where a decision is risky. A position far from its liquidation boundary may be decided by a bonded heuristic claim (Tier~0), one approaching it by a bonded, salted commitment to a registered model's output (Tier~1), and one at the boundary only by an on-chain-verified Halo2 zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive argument of knowledge (zk-SNARK) of the model's inference (Tier~2). Cheap claims remain accountable: for one hour anyone can slash a false claim with a proof, the bond scales with the base fee so that slashing always pays, challenges are committed before they are revealed, and a Tier-1 claimant must open its commitment or forfeit its bond. Only a verified Tier-2 approval lets the liquidator act.

Tiering gives power to whoever chooses the tier and supplies the model's inputs. TrustTier removes that party: the contract reads collateral, debt and prices from the lending protocol and Chainlink-style feeds, computes the distance to the boundary itself, refuses a tier too weak for it (with bands that widen with the collateral's volatility), and accepts a proof only if its public inputs equal the features it computed in the same transaction and the Poseidon hash of the weights it used equals the registered model's.

We implement nine Solidity contracts, an EZKL proving pipeline, a router and a decentralized application (DApp), and evaluate them with real proofs and measured gas. The model is trained on 57,081 real Aave~V2 position-days of 1,297 borrowers, read from an archive node and labelled by whether the account became liquidatable within a week. On those positions tiering saves 66\% of decision gas on ordinary borrowers, and the proven model approves 59\% fewer soft liquidations than a distance-only rule while flagging 228 of 386 accounts a day or more before they became liquidatable (median six days). Against on-chain inference (Alhaidari et al., AFT 2025) with the same model and inputs, TrustTier makes the same decisions, keeps the weights private and costs the same for any model size, whereas on-chain inference is 11\% cheaper for our 57-weight model but dearer from 113 weights on. Live fees put a year of daily decisions at \$\leTtLone{} on Ethereum and \$\leTtBaseYr{} on Base. A 60\,M-gas Ethereum block holds 166 decisions in the real tier mix (216 Tier-0 or 59 Tier-2 alone), and a real parallel EVM executes such a block \pevmHeadline{} faster than sequentially on four threads.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Lending protocols in decentralized finance (DeFi), such as Aave, Compound and MakerDAO hold billions of dollars of collateral, and a liquidation sells part of it at a discount. In the four largest protocols, 28,138 liquidations sold 807.46 million US dollars (USD) of collateral~\cite{qin2021liquidations}. Whether a position is truly at risk is increasingly judged by a model that looks beyond the loan-to-value ratio (LTV), at volatility, price momentum and account history. Today that model runs on an operator's server. Borrowers, liquidators and auditors see only the outcome, and must trust that the score came from the claimed model, on the real inputs, and that neither the model nor the record was quietly changed.

\paragraph{Why a model at all.} Aave's rule (health factor below 1) is deterministic and free, so any alternative must earn its cost. Bad debt arises in the minutes between a position becoming unsafe and a keeper acting on it, and only a signal that looks past the current LTV, at how fast prices move, can close that gap. Section~\ref{sec:eval-hf} measures the trade on identical price paths: fewer forced liquidations and insolvencies, paid for in decision gas and some unnecessary soft liquidations.

Recent work shows that such decisions \emph{can} be verified on-chain, either by running a small machine learning (ML) model inside a contract~\cite{alhaidari2025onchain} or by verifying a zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive argument of knowledge (zk-SNARK) of its inference~\cite{chen2024zkml,kang2022scaling}. The obstacle is cost. In our implementation a proof-verified liquidation decision costs 1,004,422 gas and about 10\,s of proving, 3.7 times a bonded heuristic decision. Spending that on every position is wasteful, because most positions are nowhere near their liquidation boundary.

\paragraph{The idea.} TrustTier spends verification where it matters. It borrows the intuition of memory tiering, where hot data gets the expensive fast tier: positions far from the boundary get cheap, bonded claims, and only positions at the boundary must carry a zero-knowledge proof. The cheap tiers are optimistic, as in optimistic rollups~\cite{kalodner2018arbitrum} and optimistic ML (opML)~\cite{conway2024opml}, but a dispute is settled by a single zk-SNARK rather than an interactive bisection game.

\paragraph{The catch, and our answer.} Tiering creates a new trust problem. Our first design let an off-chain router compute $\delta$ and choose the tier, and a second let a ``feature oracle'' commit each position's inputs. A reviewer put the problem precisely: \emph{the router is an unaudited operator, and the chain cannot check $\delta$}. Whoever supplies the inputs controls which tier applies and what the proof proves. This paper's central design change removes that party entirely. The contract reads collateral, debt and the liquidation threshold from the lending protocol, volatility and 24-hour momentum from Chainlink feeds, and account history from protocol timestamps; it quantizes them exactly as the circuit does, enforces the tier from its own $\delta$, and requires the proof's public inputs to equal its own features. We call this \emph{verified inputs instead of trusted inputs}. The same reasoning applies to the model itself: a proof whose weights are private but unconstrained proves that \emph{some} network said ``high risk''. The circuit therefore exposes a Poseidon hash of the weights, and the contract accepts only the registered one.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Risk-proportional verification}: three tiers whose volatility-adaptive bands the contract enforces from inputs it reads itself, and proofs bound to both the position's on-chain features and the registered model's weights (Sections~\ref{sec:design-tiers}--\ref{sec:design-inputs}).
  \item \emph{An accountable optimistic path}: feature snapshots, a lifecycle in which a proof supersedes any pending cheap claim, gas-scaled and deposit-funded bonds, must-open commitments, commit-reveal challenges, and a liquidator that executes each approval once (Sections~\ref{sec:design-challenge}--\ref{sec:design-concurrency}).
  \item \emph{A complete system}: nine contracts, an EZKL/Halo2 circuit, an Aave~V3 adapter, timelocked governance, a router, a sentinel and a decentralized application (DApp) (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).
  \item \emph{An evaluation with real proofs, measured gas and real data}: 57,081 Aave~V2 position-days for training and replay, a head-to-head comparison with on-chain inference (AFT 2025) on the same model and inputs, live L1 and L2 fees, the health-factor rule as baseline, Ethereum's base layer (Layer~1, L1) versus a Layer-2 (L2) rollup, ablations, band sweeps, a study of why the model is small, block capacity, and concurrency measured on a real parallel EVM (Section~\ref{sec:eval}); and a security analysis with static analysis and fuzzing (Section~\ref{sec:security}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.} TrustTier governs the \emph{decision} that a liquidation is justified and gates its execution: in the demo lending pool, only \code{TrustTierLiquidator}, and only on an open Tier-2 approval, can liquidate. It does not replace a protocol's own health-factor liquidations, which remain the fallback. The system runs on a local Ethereum chain (Hardhat) and is deployed to the Sepolia testnet by a tested workflow.

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Liquidations.} A lending position is liquidatable when its LTV exceeds the market's liquidation threshold $\theta$. Liquidation designs regularly sell more collateral than needed; Aave~V2 had accumulated up to USD~87.4\,K of bad debt by April 2021; and 73.97\% of liquidations paid an above-average transaction fee because liquidator bots race each other~\cite{qin2021liquidations}. Small price moves can make large volumes of positions liquidatable at once~\cite{perez2021knife}. Decisions near the boundary are therefore both the most contested and the most consequential.

\paragraph{zkML.} Zero-knowledge machine learning (zkML) proves that a model produced an output. A zk-SNARK lets a prover convince a verifier that a computation produced an output without revealing private inputs~\cite{groth2016size,gabizon2019plonk}. EZKL~\cite{ezkl} compiles an Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) model into a Halo2 (PLONKish) circuit with Kate--Zaverucha--Goldberg (KZG) polynomial commitments~\cite{kate2010kzg} and emits a Solidity verifier; the proof is small and checked by a pairing computation. Proving cost grows with model size~\cite{peng2025survey,chen2024zkml}, which is why TrustTier uses a deliberately small 5$\to$8$\to$1 network (Section~\ref{sec:eval-modelsize} measures what larger ones cost and buy).

\paragraph{Three observations.} (1) \emph{Risk is concentrated}: in our 374-position evaluation set only 144 positions (38.5\%) are within $\delta\le0.05$ of their threshold, so proving every position wastes most of the cost. (2) \emph{Blockchains already provide the right primitives}: immutable logs give non-repudiation, consensus order resolves races between bots, contract-held bonds make cheap claims costly to fake, and Chainlink-style feeds and protocol state are readable on-chain, so inputs need not be trusted. (3) \emph{Liquidations come in bursts}: when prices fall, many positions cross the boundary within the same few blocks, so the contracts should not stop a parallel execution engine from processing those decisions concurrently~\cite{gelashvili2023blockstm}.

\paragraph{Verifiable computation and zkML.} Pinocchio~\cite{parno2013pinocchio} made general-purpose verifiable computation practical, and pairing-based SNARKs~\cite{groth2016size,gabizon2019plonk} brought verification to a few hundred thousand gas. zkCNN~\cite{liu2021zkcnn} and Mystique~\cite{weng2021mystique} specialize proofs to neural-network inference, and ZKML~\cite{chen2024zkml} and Kang et al.~\cite{kang2022scaling} compile models to Halo2 circuits; EZKL~\cite{ezkl}, which we use, does so from ONNX and emits a Solidity verifier. These works make one proof cheaper; TrustTier instead decides which decisions need a proof at all.

\paragraph{Optimistic and hybrid verification.} TrueBit~\cite{teutsch2019truebit} and Arbitrum~\cite{kalodner2018arbitrum} accept off-chain results unless a bonded challenger wins an interactive verification game, and opML~\cite{conway2024opml} applies the same idea to ML inference. opp/ai~\cite{so2024oppai} combines optimistic and zero-knowledge ML, proving the privacy-sensitive part of a model and executing the rest optimistically. TrustTier also mixes the two, but splits by the \emph{risk of the decision} rather than by parts of the model, settles a dispute with a single non-interactive proof, and judges it on an on-chain snapshot of the inputs.

\paragraph{Lending, liquidations and attacks.} Gudgeon et al.~\cite{gudgeon2020loanable} and Bartoletti et al.~\cite{bartoletti2021lending} model lending pools, their interest rates and their liquidation mechanics, and Gudgeon et al.~\cite{gudgeon2020crisis} stress-test them, showing how a price crash and congestion leave protocols undercollateralized. Qin et al.~\cite{qin2021liquidations} and Perez et al.~\cite{perez2021knife} measure liquidation incentives, over-liquidation and instability; these are the losses an earlier, verifiable decision targets. Daian et al.~\cite{daian2020flashboys} and Qin, Zhou and Gervais~\cite{qin2022bev} show that the public mempool lets bots front-run and reorder transactions, so liquidators race for the same position; TrustTier admits one decision per position and makes losing attempts revert cheaply (Section~\ref{sec:eval-race}).

\paragraph{Oracles.} Every on-chain input of TrustTier comes from the lending protocol or a price feed. Decentralized oracle networks such as Chainlink~\cite{breidenbach2021chainlink} aggregate many reporters, but feeds can still be stale or manipulated: Eskandari et al.~\cite{eskandari2021oracles} systematize oracle designs and their manipulation, Qin et al.~\cite{qin2021flashloans} show flash-loan attacks that move on-chain prices within a transaction, and Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2023sok} catalogue such attacks across DeFi. In TrustTier a manipulated feed could move a position between tiers, so the contract checks staleness and sequencer uptime and relies on the oracle for manipulation resistance (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Concurrent execution of smart contracts.} Dickerson et al.~\cite{dickerson2017adding} introduced speculative execution by miners, who publish a schedule that validators replay. Anjana et al.~\cite{anjana2019efficient} execute a block with optimistic software transactional memory and ship a block graph for parallel validation; OptSmart~\cite{anjana2022optsmart} reduces its space cost. Saraph and Herlihy~\cite{saraph2020empirical} measured on historical Ethereum blocks how much speculative execution helps, and found that conflicts concentrate on a small amount of popular state. Block-STM~\cite{gelashvili2023blockstm} runs in production with multi-version memory and a preset order, ParallelEVM~\cite{lin2025parallelevm} detects conflicts at the level of individual operations, and Conthereum~\cite{chahoki2025conthereum} schedules conflicting transactions in advance. BlockRaFT~\cite{piduguralla2026blockraft} scales a single blockchain node out by running it as a Raft-coordinated cluster whose leader hands work to followers; TrustTier's router borrows that leader/worker shape for its own bottleneck, Tier-2 proving (Section~\ref{sec:eval-proving}). All of them gain only when transactions touch disjoint state, which is why TrustTier keys every write by position, account or model, and why we trace real read/write sets rather than assume them.

\paragraph{Contract security and proof systems.} Atzei et al.~\cite{atzei2017survey} classify attacks on Ethereum contracts, including reentrancy and failed sends; TrustTier answers them with checks-effects-interactions (CEI) and pull payments. KZG commitments~\cite{kate2010kzg} need a trusted setup; zero-knowledge scalable transparent arguments of knowledge (zk-STARKs)~\cite{bensasson2019stark} remove it at the price of much larger proofs~\cite{nainwal2025comparative}, a trade-off Section~\ref{sec:design-stark} quantifies.

\section{Threat Model and Goals}
\label{sec:threat}

\paragraph{Actors.} \emph{Borrowers} own positions in a lending protocol. \emph{Liquidators} (people or bots) submit decisions and may be dishonest: they may claim a cheaper tier than a position deserves, submit forged or replayed proofs, front-run each other, or try to lock a position to delay its liquidation. The \emph{router} is our reference liquidator and gets no special rights. \emph{Watchers} (any user of the DApp, or the sentinel) challenge false claims. \emph{Governance} is an M-of-N multisignature wallet (multisig: any M of its N owners must sign) behind a timelock.

\paragraph{Trust assumptions.} We trust exactly what the lending protocol already trusts: its own accounting (collateral, debt, threshold) and its price and volatility feeds, which must be fresh. We trust the soundness of Halo2 with KZG over BN254 under the public perpetual-powers-of-tau structured reference string (SRS), whose ceremony is secure if any one participant was honest, and that governance changes are visible a full delay before they apply. We do \emph{not} trust the router, any liquidator, or any off-chain computation of features or $\delta$.

\paragraph{Goals.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt]
  \item[G1 Input integrity.] Every decision is made on features derived from on-chain state; no party can substitute its own.
  \item[G2 Tier soundness.] No decision uses a tier weaker than the position's on-chain $\delta$ allows.
  \item[G3 Proof binding.] A Tier-2 proof approves a liquidation only for the position, and the state, it was made on, only if it was made with the registered model's weights, and only if it proves high risk.
  \item[G4 Accountability.] A false cheap claim can be slashed; an honest one cannot.
  \item[G5 Liveness.] No claim can delay a needed liquidation, and no position can be locked forever.
  \item[G6 Exactly once.] Racing liquidators finalize a position at most once, and a race over one position never delays a decision about another.
  \item[G7 Governed change.] No single key can change what the system trusts.
  \item[G8 Incentive compatibility.] Slashing a false claim pays for itself at any gas price, a claimant cannot keep its committed score hidden, and a challenge seen in the mempool cannot be stolen.
\end{description}

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Tiers}
\label{sec:design-tiers}

TrustTier sets the evidence a decision must carry by the position's distance from its boundary, $\delta = \theta - \mathrm{LTV}$, where $\theta$ is the position's own liquidation threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:bands}, Table~\ref{tab:tiers}). Tier~0 requires $\delta > B_0(\sigma)$, Tier~1 requires $\delta > B_1(\sigma)$, and Tier~2 is always allowed: a stronger tier than necessary is never refused.

\paragraph{Volatility-adaptive bands.} A cheap claim is honest only while the position stays clear of its threshold until someone could act on it, and how far a position can move in that time depends on its collateral. The bands therefore grow with the collateral's annualized 30-day volatility $\sigma$, the same feature the model sees:
\begin{align*}
B_0(\sigma) &= \mathrm{clamp}(5\% + 0.3\,\sigma,\ 10\%,\ 30\%),\\
B_1(\sigma) &= \mathrm{clamp}(1.5\% + 0.1\,\sigma,\ 3\%,\ 12\%).
\end{align*}
The slopes are move sizes: $0.1\sigma \approx 2\sigma\sqrt{1/365}$ is a two-standard-deviation one-day move, and $0.3\sigma \approx 2.2\sigma\sqrt{7/365}$ a one-week move, each plus a fixed buffer; the clamps keep a calm market from collapsing the bands and a wild one from making Tier~0 unreachable. At $\sigma=35\%$ the bands are the earlier fixed $15\%$ and $5\%$; stable collateral at $\sigma=10\%$ gets $10\%$ and $3\%$, and a 60\%-volatility asset $23\%$ and $7.5\%$. The contract computes them in integer basis points from the quantized volatility (\code{tierBands(q\_vol)}), and the router and DApp mirror it bit for bit. Section~\ref{sec:eval-bands} compares them with fixed bands.

\subsection{Verified inputs instead of trusted inputs}
\label{sec:design-inputs}

\paragraph{Where every feature comes from.} The engine's view function \code{currentFeatures(positionId, pastRoundId)} computes the model's five features from on-chain sources only (Table~\ref{tab:features}). Positions are read through a small interface, \code{IPositionSource}, which returns collateral and debt (USD with 8 decimals), the liquidation threshold, the opening and last top-up times, and the market's two feeds. We implement it twice: \code{AaveV3PositionSource} reads Aave~V3's \code{getUserAccountData}~\cite{aavev3ipool}, and \code{MockLendingPool} values demo positions with Chainlink-compatible feeds.

The first feature is \emph{utilization}, LTV divided by the position's own threshold, rather than LTV itself: markets with different thresholds are then on one scale, and on real Aave data a model on raw LTV ranked positions worse than $\delta$ alone (held-out ROC-AUC 0.86 against 0.91), while the same model on utilization ranks them better (0.92). Each feature is min-max normalized to $[0,1]$ over declared bounds and scaled by $2^{10}$, the circuit's input scale, so the integer $q$ is exactly what the circuit sees ($q/1024$). Out-of-range values are clamped, never rejected, so no position becomes undecidable; a timestamp in the future counts as zero days. An off-chain mirror in Python reproduces the arithmetic bit for bit, and tests check it against the contract and against the public inputs of real proofs.

\paragraph{The 24-hour round.} Momentum needs the price that was in effect 24 hours ago, which a contract cannot search for cheaply. The caller passes \code{pastRoundId}; the contract checks that this round was updated at or before $t-24\text{h}$ and that the next round (if any) was updated after it, so exactly one round is acceptable. The router finds it with an exponential walk back from the latest round followed by binary search over round timestamps. Chainlink proxy round IDs encode a phase in their upper bits and are consecutive only within a phase~\cite{chainlinkhistorical}, so the search stays within the current phase. The last round of a phase has no successor; the contract then accepts it only if it was updated within one heartbeat (\code{maxPriceAge}) of the target, which an older, superseded round cannot be. A wrong round can only make the transaction revert (\code{BadMomentumRound}), never mis-price it. Both feeds must also be fresh (\code{maxPriceAge}, \code{maxVolatilityAge}; 3\,h and 48\,h by default) and positive, and on an L2 an optional Chainlink sequencer-uptime feed must report the sequencer up for at least an hour.

\paragraph{Tier enforcement.} Every \code{decide*} call computes $\delta$ in basis points from the protocol's own collateral, debt and threshold and reverts with \code{TierNotAllowed(positionId, tier, deltaBps)} if the requested tier is too weak. A refused attempt does not consume the position. The router uses the same thresholds (a test reads them from the Solidity source), so it never attempts a tier the chain would refuse; it may choose a stronger tier, which only costs it gas.

\paragraph{Public features, committed model.} The circuit's five feature inputs are \emph{public} instances; the model weights stay private but are \emph{committed}. The proof's instances are $[h_W,\ q_1,\dots,q_5,\ \ell,\ \lfloor 2^{10}P(\text{low})\rceil,\ \lfloor 2^{10}P(\text{high})\rceil]$, where $h_W$ is the Poseidon hash of the quantized weights, computed inside the circuit (EZKL's \code{hashed/public} parameter visibility), and $\ell\in\{0,1\}$ is the label. \code{decideZkProof} requires $h_W$ to equal \code{tier2WeightsHash}, $\text{instances}[1..5]$ to equal the $q$ it computes in the same transaction, $\ell=1$, the cited model to be both registered and the one the verifier encodes, and the verifier to accept. A proof made for another position, or for this position before its price, collateral or debt changed, fails with \code{ProofNotForPosition}; one made with any other weights fails with \code{WrongModelWeights}. Publishing the features costs no privacy, because they are public chain data already; publishing $h_W$ reveals nothing about the weights.

\paragraph{Why the weights must be bound.} EZKL's verifier encodes the circuit's \emph{structure}, a 5$\to$8$\to$1 network, not its weights when those are private. Our earlier design registered the ONNX file's keccak256 hash and trusted that proofs used it, but nothing tied the two: we built a proof with forged weights that label every position ``high risk'', and the original verifier accepted it. With $h_W$ public, the same forged proof is rejected. The binding costs one extra public instance, 4.7\,KB of verifier code (20,361 bytes, still under the EIP-170 limit) and about 124\,k gas per verification (Table~\ref{tab:proving}). \code{setTier2Verifier(verifier, modelHash, weightsHash)} sets the verifier, the registry hash and the weights hash together, under the timelock.

\subsection{Bonded challenge window with feature snapshots}
\label{sec:design-challenge}

Tier~0/1 decisions are optimistic. The claimant posts a bond (Section~\ref{sec:design-incentives}), which opens a one-hour window. The contract stores $\mathrm{snap} = \mathrm{keccak256}(q_1,\dots,q_5)$, the features the claim was made on, and emits them in \code{FeaturesSnapshotted}. Anyone holding a valid Tier-2 proof with the registered weights whose features hash to $\mathrm{snap}$ and whose label is ``high risk'' can call \code{challengeDecision} within the window and is credited the bond. Otherwise \code{reclaimBond} credits it back to the claimant after the window. Because a zk-SNARK proves the claim wrong in one transaction, no interactive bisection game is needed.

Judging the challenge on the snapshot gives the two properties goal G4 asks for. An \emph{honest} claimant is not slashed because prices moved after the claim: a proof on later features does not match the snapshot. A \emph{false} claimant is slashed even if they top up the position afterwards: the snapshot still records the facts at claim time. Both are tested with real proofs. Payouts go to \code{pendingWithdrawals} and are pulled with \code{withdraw}, so a hostile recipient can only strand its own funds.

The Tier-1 commitment is $\mathrm{keccak256}(q, \mathrm{score}\cdot10^6, \mathrm{salt})$ with a random 32-byte salt, so the score cannot be brute-forced from the hash while the claim is fresh. The claimant must open it with \code{revealCommitment} inside the window: the contract recomputes the hash and checks that $q$ is the snapshot. A committed score of 0.5 or more contradicts the claim itself, so the bond is forfeited and the position re-opens; an unopened commitment is forfeited when the window closes. Tier~1 is therefore stronger than Tier~0 on-chain, not only for an auditor: the claimant is bound to a specific score from a registered model and must publish it.

\subsection{Position lifecycle}
\label{sec:design-lifecycle}

Figure~\ref{fig:lifecycle} shows the states a position moves through. Three rules give goals G5 and G6.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One cheap claim at a time.} While a claim's window is open, further Tier~0/1 claims revert (\code{PositionAlreadyFinalized}). The lock is the first thing every \code{decide*} checks, before any external call, so a losing racer pays only $\approx$26\,k gas.
  \item \textbf{Tier~2 supersedes.} A Tier-2 proof that the position is high risk \emph{now} is accepted even while a cheap claim's window is open (\code{ClaimSuperseded}). The superseded claim keeps its bond, snapshot and window: it is still slashed if it was false when made, and reclaimed if not. A claim therefore cannot delay a needed liquidation.
  \item \textbf{Re-opening.} A successful challenge re-opens the position so the challenger's proof can liquidate it. After an unchallenged window, the next decision re-opens it and settles the old bond. A Tier-2 approval blocks every other decision until the liquidator executes it or \code{APPROVAL\_TTL} (30 minutes) passes; either way the position, partly repaid or not, can then be decided again.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Incentives}
\label{sec:design-incentives}

An optimistic tier is secure only if someone checks it. A watcher slashes a false claim only when the bond it wins exceeds the challenge's cost, about 0.80\,M gas: with a fixed bond of 0.01 ether (ETH) that stops being true above a base fee of 12.4\,gwei ($10^{-9}$\,ETH) (Section~\ref{sec:eval-incentives}), so at ordinary L1 gas prices nobody has a reason to watch, the verifier's dilemma. TrustTier closes the gap in four ways.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gas-scaled bonds.} A claim must post $\max(0.01\,\mathrm{ETH},\ 1.5\,\mathrm{M}\cdot\mathrm{basefee})$, read from \code{block.basefee} at the claim. The bond covers a challenge at any gas price and stays profitable while the base fee at challenge time is below 1.86$\times$ the base fee at the claim.
  \item \textbf{Rewards for honest work.} The lending protocol funds a reward pool; an unchallenged claim earns \code{claimReward} when its bond is settled, at most once per position per day, so attesting to safe positions is paid work and cannot be farmed by re-claiming.
  \item \textbf{Commit-reveal challenges.} A challenger first commits $\mathrm{keccak256}(\mathrm{challenger}, \mathrm{position}, \mathrm{snap}, \mathrm{keccak256}(\mathrm{proof}))$ and reveals the proof at least one block later. Once any commit exists for a claim, the one-step \code{challengeDecision} is closed for it, so a bot that copies a revealed proof from the mempool has no commit and cannot claim the bond.
  \item \textbf{Must-open commitments} (Section~\ref{sec:design-challenge}) make a Tier-1 claim self-checking.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Executing decisions}
\label{sec:design-consumer}

A decision nobody acts on is only a record. \code{TrustTierLiquidator} is the lending protocol's one path to liquidation: \code{liquidate(position)} calls \code{consumeApproval}, which only the configured liquidator may call and which succeeds only on an open, unexpired Tier-2 approval, then asks the protocol to execute. The demo pool performs a \emph{soft} liquidation: it closes 50\% of the debt and moves collateral worth the repaid debt plus a 5\% bonus to whoever called, which pays the keeper for the proof. One proof buys exactly one liquidation. Because the model is proven on-chain, the pool may act \emph{before} LTV reaches the threshold, which is the reason to use a model at all; the protocol's own health-factor liquidation stays in force as a fallback.

\paragraph{Slow proofs.} A Tier-2 proof takes about 10\,s, longer than a block, and a feed update in between changes $q$ and invalidates it. Anyone may \code{anchorFeatures(position)}, which stores the hash of the current $q$ and $\delta$. For 10 minutes a proof on the anchored features is accepted, but only while the position has not become safer than at the anchor ($\delta_\mathrm{now}\le\delta_\mathrm{anchor}$). The router anchors only when a feed moved during proving, and then proves once more.

\subsection{Governance and model registry}

\code{ModelRegistry} holds the set of trusted model hashes; a revoked hash cannot be revoked again or silently re-registered. \code{setTier2Verifier(verifier, modelHash, weightsHash)} binds the verifier to the one model it encodes and to that model's weights, and Tier-2 decisions must cite exactly that hash and carry exactly those weights. All owner functions (registering or revoking models, swapping the verifier, changing the position source or feed staleness limits) belong to an OpenZeppelin \code{TimelockController}~\cite{ozTimelock}, whose only proposer and executor is an M-of-N multisig. Every change therefore needs M signatures and a public delay (goal G7).

\subsection{Designing for concurrent execution}
\label{sec:design-concurrency}

Parallel execution engines gain only when transactions touch disjoint state~\cite{gelashvili2023blockstm,saraph2020empirical}. TrustTier follows one rule: every storage write is keyed by a single position, account or model. Our first audit log broke it by appending every decision to one array, whose length slot every transaction wrote; \code{TierAuditLog} now stores one record per position and history is read from events. Per-position engine state is packed into three slots (claimant and bond; deadline and three flags; snapshot), which also saves about 46\,k gas per cheap claim.

Opcode traces of whole blocks (Section~\ref{sec:eval-traces}) exposed one more shared write that storage keys do not show: a bond paid as \code{msg.value} changes the engine's ETH balance. On an engine that treats a balance change as a read-modify-write, every cheap claim in a block then conflicts with every other. We therefore added \emph{deposit-funded bonds}: \code{deposit()} pre-funds the claimant's own balance in \code{pendingWithdrawals}, a claim sent without ETH draws \code{MIN\_BOND} from it, and reclaimed or slashed bonds are recycled the same way. Such a claim touches only its claimant's slot, for 5,238 extra gas. Rewards are paid when a bond is settled, not when a claim is made, so the reward pool's shared balance is never written by a claim.

\subsection{Why a SNARK, not a STARK}
\label{sec:design-stark}

zk-STARKs need no trusted setup and resist quantum attacks~\cite{bensasson2019stark}, so they are the natural alternative for Tier~2. The right choice depends on how proofs are used, and TrustTier verifies proofs one at a time, inside the liquidation that depends on them. Published measurements favour a SNARK for that pattern (Table~\ref{tab:stark}). For the same statement, a STARK proof was 68,564 bytes against 384 bytes for Groth16, about 178 times larger~\cite{nainwal2025comparative}. Verifying one StarkWare proof on Ethereum costs about 9\,M gas, affordable only because one proof covers a whole batch~\cite{epicweb3packing}. A Cairo proof of a tiny Fibonacci program is 166\,KB~\cite{zksecurityadapter}. Even STARK-based production systems wrap their STARK in a Groth16 proof for the on-chain step~\cite{risczerorecursion}. Our Halo2 proof is 4,480 bytes and verifies for 745,517 gas. It is larger than a Groth16 proof because Halo2/KZG uses a universal, circuit-independent setup, and EZKL turns an ONNX model into a circuit and verifier directly~\cite{ezkl}. We accept a trusted setup and no post-quantum security; if Tier-2 decisions were batched into a periodic settlement proof, a STARK's economics would win.

\paragraph{Contracts.} \code{LiquidationEngine} (15,026 bytes deployed), \code{TrustTierLiquidator}, \code{ModelRegistry}, \code{TierAuditLog}, the EZKL-generated \code{Halo2Verifier} (20,361 bytes, under the 24,576-byte contract-size limit of Ethereum Improvement Proposal (EIP) 170~\cite{eip170}), \code{AaveV3PositionSource}, \code{MockLendingPool}, \code{TrustTierMultisig} and \code{TrustTierTimelock}, in Solidity 0.8.24 with OpenZeppelin~5. Every revert is a custom error. EZKL's verifier reverts on a bad proof instead of returning false, so the engine wraps it in \code{try/catch}. The Aave adapter cannot read account age or top-up times from Aave, which stores neither; it records registration time and lets anyone \code{poke} an account to record a top-up when its aToken's \emph{scaled} balance (deposits without accrued interest) grows. Both are derived from on-chain state only.

\paragraph{Risk model.} A 5$\to$8$\to$1 multilayer perceptron (scikit-learn) on the five normalized features, exported to ONNX with logits clipped to $\pm8$ inside the graph. It is trained on real outcomes: 40,651 Aave~V2 position-days of 1,176 borrowers that were not yet liquidatable, each labelled 1 if the account became liquidatable (health factor below 1) within the next seven days (Section~\ref{sec:eval-real}). Accounts, not rows, are split 75/25 between training and test, so no borrower appears in both, and the rare positives (7.1\%) are oversampled in training only. On the held-out accounts accuracy is 90.9\%, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) 0.921, precision 0.42 and recall 0.66. The model's keccak256 hash is what \code{ModelRegistry} stores, and the Poseidon hash of its quantized weights what the engine checks.

\paragraph{Circuit.} EZKL~23.0.5 with input and parameter scale $2^{10}$, public inputs, Poseidon-hashed parameters (\code{hashed/public}) and public outputs, $2^{16}$ rows, and one inner column (\code{num\_inner\_cols=1}), which keeps the verifier under the EIP-170 limit. Hashing the five \emph{inputs} instead would not fit, but hashing the weights does: the hash is one public instance. EZKL's witness generation hangs on inputs outside its calibrated range, so we calibrate over the whole normalized domain including all corners, and check circuit/ONNX label agreement on 200 random feature vectors (0 disagreements). The 8.4\,MB SRS is the public $2^{16}$ powers of the perpetual-powers-of-tau ceremony~\cite{ezkl}, fetched with EZKL's \code{get\_srs}, which checks its hash. EZKL's setup is deterministic for a given SRS, so the 785\,MB proving key is not shipped and is rebuilt in 10\,s, with a check that it matches the shipped verification key and verifier.

\paragraph{Router, sentinel and DApp.} The FastAPI router reads the engine's own view of a position, picks the weakest tier the chain accepts, runs the model, and escalates a Tier-1 position to Tier~2 when the model flags it, since a cheap ``safe'' claim on it would be false and slashable. A watcher endpoint builds a Tier-2 proof on a claim's snapshot (read from \code{FeaturesSnapshotted}). The React DApp signs users in with Sign-In with Ethereum (SIWE; Ethereum Request for Comments (ERC) 4361~\cite{eip4361}); users open demo positions, see the contract's features, $\delta$, allowed tier and the model's verdict, push mock prices, run a five-step guided demo, watch open claims and pending approvals with live countdowns, and challenge (commit, then reveal), settle bonds, execute liquidations and withdraw from their own wallets. Users can generate an Ethereum account in the browser (a Bitcoin Improvement Proposal 39 (BIP-39) recovery phrase and a password-encrypted keystore that never leaves the device) or bring MetaMask; after signing in with Ethereum, the account signs its own claims, challenges, Tier-2 decisions (with a proof from the router) and liquidations. A SQLite ledger indexes every contract event with its sender and block time and records every action requested through the router; it is an index of the chain, rebuilt from it at any time, and a restarted local chain opens a new epoch instead of mixing runs. A GitHub Actions workflow deploys to Sepolia, verifies the sources on Etherscan and publishes the addresses; the hosted DApp then reads the contracts directly over a public remote procedure call (RPC) endpoint with no server. When no chain is reachable, it runs the contract's rules, the real model (exported weights, checked against ONNX) and the measured gas in the browser, clearly labelled as a simulation. The sentinel polls events and raises a critical alert when a challenge succeeds. There is no database: on-chain events are the history.

\paragraph{Prover pool.} Inspired by BlockRaFT~\cite{piduguralla2026blockraft}, the router acts as a leader that hands Tier-2 proofs to workers: local processes, each with its own share of the cores, or prover workers on other machines reached over HTTP. When a batch of positions arrives (a crash), cheap claims are submitted at once while every proof runs in parallel; Tier-2 decisions are then submitted in order from the router's one account. Workers need no trust, since a wrong proof only makes the router's transaction revert.

\paragraph{Testing.} 137 Hardhat tests cover every function and custom error, proofs with forged weights, real proofs against the real verifier end to end, the Aave adapter, governance, tier enforcement, snapshots, lifecycle, deposits, gas-scaled bonds, rewards, reveals, commit-reveal challenges, anchors, the liquidator, the sequencer check and a Chainlink phase boundary. 94 Python tests cover the feature mirror, the adaptive bands against real proof instances, the tier rule, commitments, the 24-hour round search (against brute force on 50 random histories), the cost model, the parallel-execution simulator and the DApp's exported model.

\subsection{Choosing the tier bands}
\label{sec:eval-bands}

The bands are the two numbers TrustTier's guarantees hang on, so we asked how much the results depend on them, first with fixed bands and then with the adaptive ones. Replaying the 374 positions over 34 fixed settings (Figure~\ref{fig:band-sensitivity}) shows that fixed values near $B_0=0.15$, $B_1=0.05$ can be argued for rather than assumed.

The Tier-1 band is a safety margin. A cheap claim is honest only while the position stays below its threshold for the one-hour window, and a claim made at $\delta > B_1$ survives a sudden collateral price drop $s$ whenever $B_1 \ge \theta s$. For $\theta=0.80$ and a severe 5\% hourly drop this requires $B_1 \ge 0.04$. The replay agrees: with $B_1=0$, 13 cheap claims would be pushed past their threshold by such a drop; with $B_1=0.02$, five; with $B_1=0.05$, none.

The Tier-0 band trades gas for model oversight. With $B_1=0.05$, widening it from 0.08 to 0.30 lowers the saving from 41.5\% to 37.8\%, while the Tier-0 claims the model would flag fall from one (at 0.08--0.12) to none from 0.15 (15 at $B_0=0.05$). Beyond 0.15 each step costs gas for no gain. $(0.15, 0.05)$ lies on the Pareto front of the grid over saving, flagged claims and exposed claims: no setting is better on all three. $B_0=0.08$ is an equally defensible fixed choice that saves 2.1 more points for one flagged claim.

The 0.80 threshold used for the synthetic stream matters less than it might appear. The contract never assumes it, and the real positions keep their own thresholds. Moving the synthetic threshold from 0.70 to 0.85 (Table~\ref{tab:threshold-sensitivity}) changes the saving from 25.6\% to 42.3\%, because the synthetic positions sit closer to or further from their boundary. The safety measures do not change: no flagged Tier-0 claim and no exposed claim at any threshold.

\paragraph{Adaptive against fixed bands.} A fixed band is right for one volatility only. On the design set the adaptive bands give 146/73/155 positions per tier and save 40.6\% (fixed: 39.4\%), with no flagged Tier-0 claim; two cheap claims on low-volatility positions, whose Tier-1 band narrows to 3\%, would be breached by the 5\% hourly drop, a move far outside what such collateral does. The real test is on real borrowers with real outcomes (Table~\ref{tab:aave}): there the adaptive bands make 27\% fewer cheap claims on ordinary borrowers that became liquidatable within a week (98 against 134) and 33\% fewer on accounts that were later liquidated (559 against 831), and 40--57\% fewer within a day, for 4 to 9 points of gas saving. The fixed bands save more gas only by vouching for positions that then failed. ``Flagged'' in the sweeps is the model's own verdict; the real-data comparison uses outcomes.

\subsection{Model calibration and fidelity}

Our first model was trained on synthetic labels centred at LTV~0.5, below almost every position, and flagged 84.1\% of Tier-0 positions as high risk. Its successor centred synthetic labels near the threshold, but synthetic labels are a formula of the model's own inputs, so its 95.9\% accuracy said little about real risk. The deployed model is trained on real outcomes (Section~\ref{sec:impl}): on held-out accounts it reaches ROC-AUC 0.921, it flags none of the 146 Tier-0 positions of the design set, and the circuit agrees with the ONNX model on all 200 random inputs, so the proven label is the model's label. Of the 155 Tier-2 positions, 15 are scored low risk; their proof shows label~0, the engine would reject the liquidation, and the router submits nothing. Proximity to the boundary alone never liquidates a position; the proven model output does.

\subsection{Against on-chain inference (Alhaidari et al., AFT 2025)}
\label{sec:eval-baseline}

They report 57,603--138,173 gas per L1 inference for linear models and an MLP, up to 1.13\,M for an RNN, and 5.75\,M gas to push an updated MLP's weights from L2 to L1 (their Table~8). Their model detects exploits from transaction features, so their accuracy numbers do not transfer to our task. It even inherits our input integrity; in their design the caller supplies the features. The integer model makes the same decision as the ONNX model, and hence as the circuit, on all 57,081 real position-days. \paragraph{Same decisions.} Every position the model flags lies inside TrustTier's Tier-2 band or is escalated into it, so tiering loses none of the decisions that running the model on every position makes: the same 838 approvals, the same 80 of them on positions that became liquidatable, the same early warnings. \paragraph{Cost: tiny models favour the baseline, larger ones do not.} For our 57-weight model, on-chain inference is the cheaper path: 281--303\,k gas per decision (92.6\,k of it reading the verified features) against TrustTier's average of 341\,k, about 11\% less in dollars on every chain. \paragraph{Privacy and gaming.} The baseline's weights are public, so anyone can compute exactly what changes a verdict. On the real data this is not hypothetical: a single top-up of any size resets the days-since-top-up feature and turns 1,403 of the 5,457 flagged position-days (26\%) into ``low risk''. Hiding the weights does not fix the feature, though: counting only top-ups above a minimum size, on-chain and in the training data, is the real remedy (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Updates.} Changing the baseline's model is a storage write per weight (1.0\,M gas for 57 weights here; 5.75\,M for their MLP's L2-to-L1 transfer). Changing TrustTier's means deploying a new verifier (4.45\,M gas) and re-pointing the engine under the timelock, the same for any model size.

\subsection{Incentives and where TrustTier pays}
\label{sec:eval-incentives}

We price the challenge game with measured gas. With a fixed 0.01\,ETH bond, slashing a false claim loses money above 12.4\,gwei (11.1\,gwei with commit-reveal): the verifier's dilemma at ordinary L1 prices. \subsection{Concurrency}
\label{sec:eval-concurrency}

We study concurrency at four levels of realism: a modelled scheduler; opcode-traced blocks executed by simulated parallel engines with 1 to 64 threads; the same blocks executed by a real parallel EVM; and real competing liquidator threads on a live node. First, though, how many decisions fit in a block at all.

\paragraph{Block capacity.}
\label{sec:eval-capacity}
How many decisions can one Ethereum block carry? We set Hardhat's block gas limit to mainnet's (60\,M gas when measured), submit more decisions of one tier than can fit from 20 accounts with mining paused, mine a single block and count what it included. Tier-2 decisions use the deployed verifier and a real proof; every position is opened with the proof's on-chain parameters, so the one proof is valid for each. A block holds 216 Tier-0 claims, 172 Tier-1 claims or 59 Tier-2 decisions (99.4\%, 99.4\% and 98.8\% full), all of which succeed. Real blocks mix tiers, so we also submitted 220 decisions in the mix TrustTier chose for real Aave borrowers (70\% Tier~0, 24\% Tier~1, 6\% Tier~2): the block took 166 of them (118, 36 and 12; 57.1\,M gas), and the next would not have fit. Ethereum limits gas, not transactions, and since the Osaka upgrade also caps one transaction at $2^{24}\approx16.8$\,M gas (EIP-7825); a Tier-2 decision uses 6\% of that, so only the block limit binds. Fifty-nine proven approvals per 12-second block is about 295 a minute, some fifty times what one 4-core prover produces (Section~\ref{sec:eval-proving}); in a crash, proving runs out long before block space does, unless proving is spread over many machines, at which point Tier-2 verification would take whole blocks and a batched settlement proof would pay.

\paragraph{Modelled scheduling.} Modelling each decision's read/write set and scheduling a batch greedily into conflict-free rounds, the array-based audit log needs one round per decision (374 rounds for 374 decisions), and per-position storage needs one round at every batch size from 50 to 2,000 and every tier mix from 80/15/5 to 10/20/70. Modelled same-position contention of 5--50\% raises this to 3--6 rounds.

\paragraph{Traced read/write sets.}
\label{sec:eval-traces}
Models can miss shared state, so we measured it. For each scenario, a tracing script deploys the contracts with three markets, opens the positions, puts a whole batch of decisions from distinct liquidator accounts into \emph{one block}, mines it, and replays every transaction with \code{debug\_traceTransaction}. From the opcode trace we record every storage slot read (\code{SLOAD}) and written (\code{SSTORE}) in each contract, following the call stack, and every account balance moved. We then simulate three ways to execute the block, using each transaction's measured gas as its duration, with Tier~2 rescaled to the real-verifier cost: \emph{strict rounds} (any read/write overlap conflicts); a \emph{pre-computed schedule}, in which the block producer publishes a dependency graph that validators replay on $T$ threads~\cite{dickerson2017adding,anjana2019efficient}; and an \emph{optimistic} Block-STM-style engine~\cite{gelashvili2023blockstm}, in which $T$ threads execute speculatively in block order, only read-after-write dependencies count, and a transaction that read a value a lower transaction later wrote is aborted and re-executed. Fees to the block producer are deferred, as in every parallel Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM). To study scaling, most traced blocks are larger than Ethereum allows: with a stub verifier, 50--374 decisions use 15--110\,M gas, and with the real verifier 100--374 decisions use 61--229\,M, up to 3.8 times today's 60\,M limit, sizes that only higher gas limits or a rollup's throughput would reach. One block, \emph{mainnet block}, is exactly what fits in 60\,M gas in the real tier mix. Bond transfers into the engine are modelled two ways: as a commutative balance delta, or as a read-modify-write of the engine's balance. Table~\ref{tab:parallel} and Figures~\ref{fig:speedup}--\ref{fig:contention} report the results.

Five findings stand out.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Per-position storage parallelizes.} A block of 374 decisions has no shared storage write at all. It needs one strict round and reaches 15.3$\times$ on 16 threads and 54.8$\times$ on 64, with the real verifier too. A block of mainnet size, 166 decisions, has fewer and more uneven transactions (12 of them Tier-2 decisions that each cost 3.7 Tier-0 ones), so it reaches 13.7$\times$ on 16 threads and 35.1$\times$ on 64. Scaling falls short of ideal only because transaction durations differ: one Tier-2 decision costs 3.3 times a Tier-0 one, so the longest transactions bound the makespan. The array audit log of our first design serializes the same block completely, whatever the engine.
  \item \textbf{The balance is the hidden hot spot.} Storage keys alone would suggest the final design is conflict-free. The traces show that every \code{msg.value} bond also writes the engine's ETH balance. Treated as a read-modify-write, that single location makes the 209 bonded claims a chain: 3.65$\times$ at any thread count, with 208 aborts in the optimistic engine. Deposit-funded bonds (Section~\ref{sec:design-concurrency}) restore 15.3$\times$ under both balance models, for 5,238 gas per claim; the real parallel EVM below confirms both effects. As on historical Ethereum, conflicts concentrate on a little popular state~\cite{saraph2020empirical}; tracing found ours where our model had not.
  \item \textbf{Racing liquidators cost little.} With 5--50\% duplicate attempts, losing attempts revert at the per-position lock, write nothing, and conflict only with the winner. The block still needs only 2 strict rounds, and 64-thread speedup stays at 42.9--52.1$\times$. Optimistic execution even beats the in-order schedule here: the schedule leaves a thread idle while it waits for a dependency, whereas the optimistic engine keeps every thread busy and pays only for a few aborts. The modelled scheduler had predicted 3--6 rounds, since it assumed losers write.
  \item \textbf{Oracle updates cause little conflict.} Chainlink heartbeat updates inside the block conflict with every later decision on that market, which reads the feed. Six updates raise strict rounds to 3 and cause 6 aborts at 16 threads, costing up to 2\% of optimistic speedup at 16 threads and 15\% at 64.
  \item \textbf{Parallelism needs many senders.} A single liquidator account submitting all 200 decisions serializes the block through its own nonce (1.0$\times$). Parallel execution helps a market of liquidators, not one bot.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Proving throughput}
\label{sec:eval-proving}

Tier-2 proving is TrustTier's bottleneck, and it matters most in a crash, when many positions cross into Tier~2 at once. We generate the same 12 real EZKL proofs under several configurations on our 4-vCPU, 16\,GB machine, after warming the workers up, and check every proof's features and label. A single proof already uses all four cores, 10.3\,s each. Splitting the cores among workers therefore trades latency for little throughput (Table~\ref{tab:pool}): two workers of two threads prove 8\% more per minute, but each proof takes 1.8 times longer, and every worker holds its own 1.9--2.2\,GB copy of the proving key; oversubscribing the cores adds at most 13\%. Scaling out is what works. With remote prover workers over HTTP, emulated as disjoint 2-core sets on the same host (so real machines can only do better), two ``machines'' prove 1.82$\times$ faster than one (3.53 $\to$ 6.42 proofs/min) at nearly unchanged latency. For the 155 Tier-2 positions of the design set, one 4-core machine needs 26.7 minutes; at the measured rate, four such machines would need about 5.9 (projected). On a live node, a batch through a 2-worker pool produced real proofs in parallel and the real verifier accepted the approvals.

\subsection{End-to-end validation}

With the real verifier on a local node, the router opens and routes streams across the demo markets and submits real Tier-2 decisions, each with $\approx$10\,s of proving; the Hardhat integration tests run the same path with the deployed verifier and real proofs, including proofs with forged weights, which are rejected. Through the DApp, in a headless browser with an injected wallet, we opened a watch-band position (on-chain $\delta=800$ basis points (bps), allowed tier~1, model: high risk), had the router claim Tier~1 dishonestly, generated a real proof on the claim's snapshot, and challenged from a second wallet. The bond was credited to the challenger, the position re-opened, and the same proof then approved its liquidation at Tier~2. Claiming Tier~0 on that position reverted with \code{TierNotAllowed(\dots, 0, 800)}. With the final contracts, the DApp's five-step guided demo runs the same story on a volatile market ($\sigma=60\%$, $\theta=0.86$, bands 23\% and 7.5\%) and then executes the soft liquidation through \code{TrustTierLiquidator}, taking $\delta$ from 8.0\% to 20.0\%; a browser test drives it end to end, on-chain and in simulation mode.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Security analysis}
\label{sec:security}

We reviewed the code with STRIDE, which sorts threats into spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service (DoS) and elevation of privilege, and with an independent review pass; the 32 findings and their status are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:stride} (Table~\ref{tab:security}). We argue the goals of Section~\ref{sec:threat} as follows.

\textbf{G1 Input integrity:} every feature is computed by the contract from the position source and feeds within the transaction that uses it (finding~15). \textbf{G2 Tier soundness:} every cheap \code{decide*} recomputes $\delta$ and reverts below its band (finding~20). \textbf{G3 Proof binding:} \code{decideZkProof} compares the proof's five feature instances with its own computation and its weights hash with the registered one, and requires label~1, a registered model and the verifier's own model (findings 9--11, 32). \textbf{G4 Accountability:} a challenge must prove high risk on the claim's snapshot (finding~15). \textbf{G5 Liveness:} Tier~2 supersedes open claims, windows expire, and future timestamps clamp (finding~17). \textbf{G6 Exactly once:} the lock is set before any external call; tested on a live node with up to 16 racing threads. \textbf{G7 Governed change:} a multisig behind a timelock owns every contract (finding~3). \textbf{G8 Incentive compatibility:} gas-scaled bonds, commit-reveal challenges and must-open commitments (findings 8, 24, 25).

\textit{Automated analysis.} Slither~\cite{feist2019slither} initially reported 13 medium and 32 low findings; we fixed the substantive ones (the verifier was called before the decision's state was written, now reordered as checks-effects-interactions at no gas cost; a division before a multiplication; missing zero-address checks) and documented the rest as intended, leaving 0 findings, enforced in continuous integration (CI). Echidna~\cite{grieco2020echidna} fuzzes the whole system through random sequences of every action by three accounts with random time jumps, and checks seven invariants after each call: the engine's ETH equals exactly what it owes, the adaptive tier bands hold, Tier~2 needs a valid high-risk proof, every accepted proof carries the registered weights hash, a slash needs a valid high-risk proof on the claim's snapshot, every liquidation consumes a fresh approval at most once, and bond bookkeeping is consistent. All seven hold over \echidnaCalls{} calls, with coverage reaching every outcome; and each of four planted bugs (no Tier-0 band, approvals that never expire, a challenge that ignores the snapshot, a bond paid twice) is caught by the property meant to catch it.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Inputs are only as sound as their feeds: the contract rejects stale data but leaves resistance to manipulation~\cite{zhou2023sok,eskandari2021oracles} to the oracle, and volatility feeds are mocked on testnets. The real data are Aave~V2 on Ethereum, with ETH's volatility standing in for each account's collateral mix and daily snapshots, so intraday crashes are seen only at day granularity; most near-boundary positions recover, and even the proven model's approvals are mostly positions that would not have become liquidatable within a week (90\% on ordinary borrowers), so a soft liquidation must stay small. Daily decisions pay for themselves on L1 only for large positions, and a bond keeps challenges profitable only while the base fee stays below 1.86$\times$ its value at the claim. The band formula is derived from move sizes, not optimized. The top-up feature counts a top-up of any size, so a borrower can reset it with dust; 26\% of flagged position-days would flip this way (Section~\ref{sec:eval-baseline}). Counting only top-ups above a minimum share of the collateral, in the contract and in the training labels, closes this, at the price of retraining. The parallel-EVM measurements execute exported blocks on in-memory state, without a client's storage I/O, and use 4 cores. The SRS comes from a public ceremony, but KZG still needs one; the weights stay private only up to what the label and probabilities reveal over many queries.

\subsection{Future work}

Four directions follow from these results. \emph{Batched settlement}: proving many Tier-2 decisions in one periodic proof would let a STARK's transparent setup and amortized verification win, and would lift the 59-proof-per-block ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:eval-capacity}. \emph{Per-asset data}: per-reserve volatility feeds and per-asset account data would replace the ETH proxy, and fitting the band formula to outcomes would replace its move-size derivation. \emph{L2 deployment}: measuring the scheme on a rollup, where Table~\ref{tab:viability} says it pays, including the sequencer check. \emph{Model governance}: retraining on fresh outcomes means new weights, hence a new weights hash and verifier; a registry of weights hashes for one circuit would avoid redeploying the verifier.

%% ===================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

TrustTier spends zero-knowledge proofs only where a liquidation decision is close enough to the boundary to need one. On real Aave~V2 borrowers that saves 66\% of decision gas, and a model trained on what actually happened to them, proven on-chain, approves 59\% fewer soft liquidations than distance alone while warning a median of six days ahead. Tiering, however, creates a party to trust: whoever chooses the tier and supplies the inputs, and, as we found, whoever chooses the weights. The final design removes both. The contract reads its own inputs, enforces bands that widen with volatility, accepts only proofs about the state it sees and made with the registered weights, and judges cheap claims on the facts at the moment they were made. Gas-scaled bonds, rewards, must-open commitments and commit-reveal challenges make watching worth someone's while, and the liquidator turns a verified decision into exactly one action. Measuring at the opcode level found the one shared write our model had missed; with it removed, a real parallel EVM executes a full Ethereum block of decisions \pevmHeadline{} faster on four threads, though a block holds at most 59 proofs. Risk-proportional verification on verified inputs and committed models is a pattern that should carry beyond liquidations, to credit scoring, insurance claims or any decision whose stakes vary.
